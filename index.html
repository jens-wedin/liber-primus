Notes on the attack against the unsolved runes — what the ciphertext is, what has been ruled out, and where the trail leads next.

The Central Finding

The unsolved ciphertext is, statistically, a uniform random stream bound by a single law — adjacent runes are almost never equal.

0.66%
observed doublet rate
3.45%
expected at random
~17σ
the deficiency

A pure lag-1 effect: first differences are otherwise uniform. It rules out substitution and every periodic or independent keystream. What survives is an output-stage no-repeat enforcement — most likely a key-skip that advances the key pointer to dodge a would-be doublet, desynchronising the stream by ~3% and defeating every fixed-position attack.

Modeled further, the collision resolution is uniform, and the 86 residual doublets carry no key-period leak. A second transcription reproduces every one of them and disagrees with ours on only a dozen runes in the whole book — so “copy-error noise” no longer holds: the doublets look like a real, imperfect edge of the no-repeat rule. Whether a re-roll pad or a key-skip, the differences stay uniform; that is why every keyed attack, on the raw stream and on the differences alike, comes back silent.

The imperfect edge now has a number. The rule is soft: it keeps a would-be doublet about one time in five — a single acceptance rate, p ≈ 0.19, that reproduces the exact 0.66% rate and leaves the stream otherwise uniform. That reframes the 86 doublets as the filter’s own leak — signal, not noise. A separate project working the same ciphertext fitted the same figure independently.

The Candidate Key-Texts

The books Cicada wrote by — all silent

Five candidate texts, each tested as a running key — whole text, every offset, both signs, and now read backwards as well as forwards, a variant the earlier work never actually covered despite claiming “both directions”. Every control passed: a key planted in that same text is recovered at ~100%. Every real decode still falls short of it.

Measuring the noise these searches reach turned up a law that bounds the whole approach: the bigger the book, the blunter the test. A long text gives the scan more offsets to cherry-pick, so pure chance climbs toward the score a genuine key would give. The gap between them — all the room this method has to work in — shrinks steadily with length.

Extrapolate and a text of a few tens of millions of runes would have no usable gap at all — chance would reach whatever a real key reaches, and the method could not tell them apart at any score. Bigger candidate texts are not merely unpromising; they are progressively untestable. The productive direction was always shorter and better motivated — and the best-motivated short text in existence is the one at the top of that list.

Every claim is control-validated — encrypt known English with the hypothesised scheme, then recover it through the same pipeline, before any result is trusted.

Auditing the Audit

Turning the method on itself

Every negative above rests on a claim of the form “if this cipher were the answer, we would have seen it.” That claim is itself testable, and when it was finally tested, several of these results turned out to be resting on nothing. Not wrong — unsupported, which is a different and more embarrassing thing.

  • ⚠A rule that would have hidden a real break. Several searches asked “does this decode score near English?” But those searches carry a penalty that puts a genuine break well below English. Planting real keys and recovering them at 97–100% accuracy produced scores the rule would have thrown away. One script would have called its own successful control a failure.
  • ⚠A test that never ran. The gematria filter gated both runs to prime lengths — but the run it was built to detect is 74 runes long, which is not prime. It counted zero real signatures and compared noise to noise.
  • ⚠A yardstick made of the thing being measured. One attack drew its “random” baseline from the very generator it was brute-forcing, then recovered it and scored the result as chance. The baseline was inflated enough to bury roughly a third of genuine breaks — and the same flaw had quietly reached the shared fix written to prevent it.
  • ⚠Coverage that wasn’t. Crib-dragging reported 32 phrases; 18 were ever really tested. “Both directions” meant both signs, never a reversed book. One running-key test searched 0.1% of its own hypothesis space.

Everything was rebuilt on two measured quantities instead of a guess: a detection floor — plant the hypothesis, recover it, and see what a real break actually scores — and a matched ceiling drawn from an independent null of the same length and the same number of trials. Where a search can’t clear its own floor, or the floor and ceiling nearly touch, the honest answer is no evidence, not a negative.

Two “possible breaks” were flagged by the new machinery and both dissolved on inspection: the winning decodes were gibberish that outscored real English of the same length. Short texts plus a flexible search will fit anything. The decode is the evidence — never the number.

A New Front

The images hold what the runes did not

A page-by-page sweep of all 75 scans overturned an earlier, hasty verdict. The transcription is faithful for the runes — colour and punctuation are provably cosmetic — but it silently drops the pages that are not runes at all.

  • ✦Two magic squares. Page 16 bears a 5×5 square of magic constant 3301 — palindromic, prime centre 809. Page 32 bears a 4×4 grid whose every cell is 3301 minus a prime, one marked in red, beside a hand-drawn Möbius.
  • ✦A page of code. Page 67 is 104 two-character codes and not one rune; pages 66, 68 and 73 carry more, in decimal and hex. None of it appears in the transcription.

Both have since been worked through. The squares are not a key, not an interrupter schedule, and not a message. The code pages were re-read from the scans — page 66 transcribed for the first time, 256 codes in all — then tried as a pad, an index, a self-cipher and a lookup table. All silent. The wall still stands — but its shape is now sharper. A pad derived from a short seed is finite, and a planted one is recovered cleanly by the beam; the wall is therefore seed entropy, not the pad idea itself. Thematic passphrase seeds were tried and fell short. What is unbreakable is a high-entropy or truly external pad — that, and only that, is the explanation left standing.

One thing was solved, though. The 256 codes were an unread object; another archive holds the same pages rendered as a 256-byte string. Reading ours in the plain order and scoring each code as digit×60 plus its base-62 letter reproduces that string byte for byte — and the derivation caught three letters mis-read as a lowercase l where a capital I was meant, one of them a byte a rival project had independently flagged as disputed. The map is closed and the transcription is now verified against an outside source. The bytes still sit behind the pad; but for once, two records were made to agree exactly.

The Trail Ahead

The backlog — run down, each with its control

A queue opened after the magic-square readings closed, then worked through in two waves — the second steered by a fresh survey of the solver community. Each item carried its own control; the marks show what survived. Every result was then re-audited adversarially, which caught real defects in this very work — a threshold that would have hidden a genuine break, a statistic that never implemented its own test. None broke the cipher — but four changed what we believe: the residual doublets look real rather than copy-noise, and now carry a measured acceptance rate; the tempting ᚠ-and-3301/1033 gematria coincidence turned out to be numerology; a long-standing claim that short keys were untestable turned out to be a bug in the test, not a property of the cipher; and the wall the runes sit behind is now known to be seed entropy, since a short-seed pad is recoverable and only a high-entropy one resists.

  • ✓The literal-ᚠ interrupter. The solved-page rule that a ciphertext ᚠ may be an unencrypted F consuming no key — a pointer hold the key-skip beam could never express. Tested: negative. It reads no better than random.
  • ✓Map the ᚠ positions. Do the literal-F runes mark structural boundaries — prime-length runs, checksums — on the unsolved pages as on the solved? Tested: no fingerprint found — though with only ~40 literal-F runes to learn from, the test is too weak to prove none exists.
  • ✓The code pages, transcribed & tested. Pages 67 and 68 confirmed exact, page 66 read at last — 256 = 28 codes. Tried as a pad, an index into the runes, a self-cipher, a lookup table: all gibberish, and the set is not a permutation. What remains is a keyed pad — the same wall the runes sit behind.
  • ✓A second transcription. rtkd/iddqd agrees with ours on all but a dozen runes in the whole book, and reproduces every one of the 86 doublets. “Copy-error noise” no longer holds — though at 99.95% agreement the two likely share a source, so “real” is favoured, not proven.
  • ✓A GP-sum plausibility filter. Built and controlled — and it fails: the 3301+1033 co-occurrence turns up just as often in shuffles of the very same runes (P = 50%). The gematria coincidence is numerology; no usable filter.
  • ✓The squares as a message. Not a key but a puzzle — the values read directly, along every path, into runes or ASCII. Page 16 is indistinguishable from its own shuffles; page 32 is only marginal (p ≈ 8%). At 16–25 symbols nothing could be decisive. Their meaning stays the 3301/prime structure.
  • ✓Prime-indexed hints. The old whitespace prime sequences, the emirp cookies 167/761, the “missing primes” — tried as keys and offsets. All gibberish; the pre-2014 hints don’t key the runes.
  • ✓A key that restarts each page. The one structural idea that could explain every failure without an unbreakable pad: if the key resets at each page or line, no whole-stream attack could ever work. The page and line breaks came from a second transcription. Ruled out decisively — and on real text the winning schedule is the one that never resets at all.
  • ✓Keys on the differences. If each rune were added to the one before it, the differences would be the message — so ordinary word keys and book keys ought to work there. They don’t, closing the chained-cipher variant of both families.
  • ✓Doubly-coined keys. FIRFUMFERENFE is one substitution deep; nothing says a setter stopped there. 250 two-transform variants. No evidence either way — at this length the search fits anything, which is the limit of the whole word-key family.
  • ✓A pad from a short seed. A hash keystream grown from a guessable passphrase is finite — and a planted one is recovered by the beam at 100% where a rigid decode reads only noise. Tested against 116 thematic seeds through four hash constructions: negative. The lane is real; these seeds are not the key.
  • ✓Hand-computable keystreams. The Gromark cipher grows a long key from a short primer by chain addition — the sort a setter could run by hand. Length-2 primers are the Fibonacci sequence, already covered; length-3 tested in full: negative.
  • ✓Reused keys, sought blindly. If two pages shared a keystream, their difference would betray it without the key — Banburismus for an irregular pointer. A power analysis says no: the signal needs ~600 aligned runes, the pages hold at most 277, and the desync caps a coherent run near 17. Untestable, not disproven.
  • ✓Forcing in the initials. A word- or line-restarting key, or an acrostic, would skew the first rune of each word or line. Word, word-final and page-initial are all uniform — the family is closed. A line-initial skew looked real, then failed against a second, image-derived segmentation: a transcription artifact.
  • ✓A parallel project, cross-checked. Another rigorous effort works the same ciphertext to within nine runes. Its ledger and ours agree on every shared negative and reached the same soft-rule figure independently — and where its book-key results were self-voided by a decoder flaw, ours, validated on the right mechanism, still stand.
  • ✓The stream as the key, not the message. Perhaps the unsolved pages are pad, not ciphertext — a running key laid against some other text, forwards, backwards or mirrored. Closed by the uniformity itself: a uniform stream minus any unrelated text stays uniform, and a folded pad would force a palindrome the stream is not. Controlled, and silent.
  • ✓A different cipher, not a different key. Every attack assumes the runes are added to a key; they might instead be multiplied and shifted — an affine cipher, which the additive searches are blind to. All 28 multipliers, over the prime, totient and word keys, through the same skip-tolerant beam: negative. Generalising the cipher fails as generalising the key did.
  • ✓Repeated passages, mined in full. A repeating key betrays itself when the same runes recur; the community had spotted a handful. Every exact repeat, weighed against a matched no-repeat null: all coincidental. The longest, a six-rune echo, is chance; its gaps share no period. No Kasiski foothold.
  • ✓A key that suppresses its own repeats. Could a famous number sequence, marching in near-constant steps, be the key that quiets the doublets on its own? No — that would leave the fingerprint of English in the differences, and the differences are flat. Sixteen such sequences, run through the beam, stay silent.
  • ✓The hints never spent. Two onion-cookie hashes and a 128-digit postscript number that Cicada left dangling, unused. Tried as keystreams, as hash seeds, and as autokey primers — digits, bytes and all: silent. The pre-2014 loose ends do not key the runes.
  • ✓Hidden ink in the scans. If a message were smuggled into the page images, its trace is a specific quirk in the file header. Only the nine instruction pages — the ones with the already-known clues — still carry that quirk; every runic page in our set was re-saved and can hold no hidden data. The one page with bytes tacked on the end is a corrupt copy, not a secret.
  • ✓Reading it the other way. Cicada mirrors its alphabet on the solved pages, so perhaps the unsolved runes should be read backwards, or reflected. Every orientation — forward, reversed, mirrored, both — through the same beam: silent. And a free reshuffling of the runes cannot even be searched, because their flat letter statistics leave nothing to steer by.
  • ✓The interrupter, on any rune. Every attack had assumed the pause-rune is ᚠ; another solver spent 38 hours letting it be any of the 29, across every key length. We read their finished results firsthand: the search recovers every solved page — and even the one solved page hiding in the unsolved set lights up exactly — yet leaves all the unsolved pages at the random floor. An independent dead end, verified.

Independently corroborated meanwhile: the community's own frequency analysis finds the unsolved stream statistically indistinguishable from random — the same verdict as the central finding above.
